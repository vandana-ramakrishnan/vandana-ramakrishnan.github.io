Tariff Attention and Inflation Expectations: Evidence from Financial Markets

With Farah Tohme

Abstract

We study how tariff attention affects market-based inflation compensation in the United States. We build a Bloomberg based tariff index to measure attention to trade policy and estimate daily local projections of Treasury Inflation-Protected Security (TIPS) breakeven inflation rates and inflation swaps at maturities from one to thirty years over July 2018 to January 2026. The market response to tariff news reverses sign between the two trade wars. In the full sample, a one-standard-deviation increase in the index is followed by declines in both measures, concentrated at short maturities and consistent with a risk-off, growth-negative channel rather than pure cost-push inflation. However it is different in the subsamples. In the first trade war (2018–2021), breakevens and swaps fall across maturities. In the second (2025-2026), inflation swaps rise across the curve while breakevens fall at the short end and turn positive at longer horizons. Because swaps are not exposed to the TIPS liquidity premium, this short-end divergence between the two instruments isolates a flight-to-liquidity effect. We find that the memory of experienced inflation is associated with higher expectations in the second trade war.

Inflation Expectations Anchoring and the State-Dependent Phillips Curve

Abstract

Does the slope of the Phillips curve depend on how firmly inflation expectations are anchored? I examine this question using an interacted vector autoregression in which inflation dynamics vary with survey-based measures of expectations anchoring. Drawing on microdata from the Survey of Professional Forecasters and the University of Michigan, I estimate the model for the United States over 1968Q4–2026Q1. I find that the cumulative inflation response to an identified demand shock is approximately three times larger when expectations are unanchored than when they are anchored. The difference reflects stronger shock transmission rather than larger demand shocks in the unanchored regime. Regional U.S. evidence also shows a steeper inflation–unemployment relationship where household expectations are less anchored, while estimates for Brazil and the euro area support the broader pattern. A historical counterfactual suggests that the same shocks experienced during 2021–23 would have raised peak core inflation to approximately 10%, rather than the observed 6.4%, under 1970s-level anchoring. The findings highlight the role of expectations anchoring in shaping inflation dynamics and the transmission of macroeconomic shocks.

Tariff News and Household Inflation Expectations: Role of Prior Beliefs

Abstract

Do major tariff announcements change household inflation expectations, and which households respond? I examine seven U.S. tariff episodes between 2018 and 2025, using Google Trends to identify peaks in public search attention and daily interview dates from the New York Fed’s Survey of Consumer Expectations (SCE) to compare within-person inflation-forecast revisions around each peak. One-year expectations rise significantly at the baseline window around the 2018 steel and aluminum tariffs (1.18 percentage points) and the July 2025 reinstatement of reciprocal tariffs (1.72 points). A separate Michigan panel records a large April 2025 revision around Liberation Day. In the two positive SCE episodes, revisions are larger among households whose previous expectations were unanchored. Low financial literacy predicts a larger response in several episodes, while measured consumption, housing and geographic tariff exposure do not systematically predict revisions. These patterns suggest an important role for prior beliefs in the transmission of policy news to expectations.

Payment Frictions and Financial Integration: The Federal Reserve’s Par Collection System

Abstract

This paper examines the role of payment frictions in financial integration in the United States, focusing on the Federal Reserve’s par collection system. Using historical bank balance sheets and county-level data, I examine banks’ transaction-fee decisions and their relationship with regional deposit patterns. The analysis employs logistic regression and causal-inference methods to investigate how differences in bank fees may have shaped banking activity and the geographic distribution of deposits.
